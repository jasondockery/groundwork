# Inclusive content design

- Prefer plain language, common words, active voice, concrete verbs, short
  sentences, and one idea at a time. Define necessary jargon at first use.
- Make errors state what happened, what remains safe, and how the user can
  recover. Never make a bare code, stack trace, color, or icon the only message.
- Avoid idioms, wordplay, and culture-specific metaphors in critical
  instructions. Keep examples respectful and varied without assuming one
  country, name shape, address, phone format, currency, holiday, or family.
- Label meaningful icons and controls. Provide appropriate text alternatives
  for images and diagrams and captions, transcripts, or equivalent content for
  audio and video.
- Never let a screenshot replace the textual procedure. Write instructions that
  remain complete without color, image access, animation, or spatial language
  such as “the green item on the right.”
- Preserve semantic headings, lists, tables, links, and reading order in web,
  document, email, and exported formats. Use descriptive link and action text.
- Mark language changes where relevant and show language names in their native
  script when practical. Load `internationalization-first` for locale, message,
  formatting, Unicode, and direction mechanics.
- Treat cognition and second-language readability as product behavior. Test
  important instructions and recovery paths with representative users or
  knowledgeable human review when the consequence warrants it.
- Keep framework components, editorial voice, pedagogy, visual branding, and
  product-specific content governance in the owning repository.

Reviewed 2026-08-10 against W3C WAI accessibility evaluation guidance and W3C
Internationalization [Quick Tips for the Web](https://www.w3.org/International/quicktips/).
